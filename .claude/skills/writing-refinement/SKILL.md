# Writing Refinement

Writing that sounds good is more likely to be right. Sound and substance aren't
separate qualities you trade off against each other — they're connected by a
rope of overlapping mechanisms, not a single rod. Compression forces you to
rethink an idea, not just reword it. Readability makes a bad step easier to
spot. Rhythm mismatched to the weight of a thought is a signal, not just an
awkward sentence. Clumsy prose is evidence of clumsy thinking — reliably
enough that fixing the sound is often the fastest way to find the flaw.

This skill treats sound as a diagnostic tool, not a finish coat. Applying it
means using each pass below to hunt for weak thinking, then fixing the idea,
not merely the phrasing around it.

**Scope.** This applies to idea-development writing — essays, arguments,
analysis, memos, proposals, anything where the author is constructing a claim.
It does not apply to describing pre-formed experimental results, raw data, or
transcripts, where the "ugly" version may simply be the accurate one.

## Process

Work through the piece section by section, not as one global pass — a catch
buried in paragraph three won't survive being averaged against a strong
paragraph one.

1. **Read aloud.** Read each sentence for sound. Flag anything awkward,
   clunky, or metronomic (every sentence the same length and shape). Note
   where the rhythm is lighter or heavier than the idea deserves — a throwaway
   rhythm on a load-bearing claim, or a dramatic cadence on a minor point.

2. **Compress.** Cut about 20% from each paragraph. Don't just delete words —
   when the cut forces a choice between two ways to keep the meaning, take the
   one that makes the claim sharper. If a paragraph won't compress without
   losing something true, that's information: the paragraph may be padded to
   hide that it doesn't say much.

3. **Find the catches.** Re-read the compressed version and mark every spot
   where something catches — a claim that doesn't quite follow from the last
   one, a transition papering over a gap, a conclusion stated with more
   confidence than the argument earns. Sound flags the location; the fix is to
   repair the idea underneath, not smooth over the sentence.

4. **Check consistency.** Read the whole piece for contradictions, unstated
   assumptions the argument depends on, and places where a term quietly
   changes meaning between its first and second use. Inconsistency is why a
   piece stops sounding beautiful even when each sentence is fine in
   isolation.

5. **Rewrite.** Produce the refined version. It should sound better and think
   better — if a change only did one of those, look harder at whether it
   actually did the other.

## Output

Return two things, in this order:

1. **The rewritten text** — clean prose in the same format as the input
   (same markup, same structure). No inline annotations, no tracked changes.
2. **Refinement notes** — 3 to 5 bullets, each one sentence, naming a thinking
   problem the rewrite fixed (not a wording problem). "Cut the passive
   construction" is not a note; "the second paragraph asserted the migration
   was low-risk without addressing the rollback gap the first paragraph had
   just raised" is.

Skip a pass's write-up if it found nothing — don't manufacture a note to hit
the count, and don't pad the rewrite to look more thorough than the input
warranted.
